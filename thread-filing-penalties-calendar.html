The October 19, 2026 update reflects the extension of the 2025 annual PAYE return to November 30, the 2024 company return date of November 30, the November VAT and VIES dates and the beneficial-ownership confirmation due by December 31.

Deadlines to lock in first

Calendar summary

October 19, 2026

The Tax Department's deadline table and announcements give the following dates for the last quarter of 2026. Where a VAT or VIES date falls on a weekend or public holiday, the table moves it to the next working day; for OSS, IOSS, CESOP and SME cross-border returns the table says the date does not move.

  • October 30: payment of defence contribution withheld on dividends or interest paid in September 2026.
  • October 31: 2025 individual income tax return for taxpayers other than the self-employed; September PAYE; GESY withheld in September; OSS and CESOP returns for July–September.
  • November 10: VAT return and payment for the July–September 2026 quarter.
  • November 16: VIES recapitulative statement for October 2026 (November 15 is a Sunday).
  • November 30: 2025 annual PAYE return (extended on September 16, 2026) and the 2024 company income tax return (IR4) and accounts-based individual return (IR1 with accounts).
  • December 31: second 2026 provisional tax installment; the deadline table notes that an extension will be given, but no new date had been published at this update.
  • December 31: annual confirmation of beneficial-owner details with the Registrar of Companies (window opened October 1; fines of up to EUR 5,000). This is a company-law filing, not a tax return.

The 2025 return for self-employed individuals who do not need audited accounts was originally due July 31, 2026. The deadline table states that an extension will be given; check the table again before relying on any date.

What VAT teams should still monitor

Operational reminder

October 19, 2026

VAT obligations change through period-specific notices, so they do not fit into one fixed annual list. Two October changes matter for retailers and suppliers. Under Order K.D.P. 354/2026, published in the Official Gazette on September 30, the zero rate on fresh, chilled or frozen meat and fish continues from October 1, 2026 to May 31, 2027. From October 12, 2026 to May 31, 2027 the zero rate also covers bread, milk, coffee, sugar and baby food. The separate zero rate on fresh fruit, vegetables, infant milk, nappies and feminine hygiene products runs to December 31, 2026; on September 30 the Cabinet approved extending it through 2027, but that needs its own implementing order.

VAT, VIES and payroll deadlines can move through separate notices and for different reasons, as the April, May and July 2026 Tax For All notices showed. Keep the calendars linked but review each one separately.

  • Review whether small enterprise VAT changes alter your status.
  • Check that till and ERP tax codes applied the October 12 product additions; prepared or flavoured items can still be outside the zero rate.
  • Track VIES separately from VAT returns; late VIES submission is shown in the latest notices with a EUR 50 penalty.
  • Do not assume payroll and VAT deadlines move together.

Penalties for late filing and late payment

Compliance note

July 16, 2026

Late filing of a tax return in Cyprus attracts a fixed charge per return, and late payment of tax due attracts interest at the statutory rate. The September 16, 2026 PAYE notice gives a concrete example under Article 50A(a) of the Assessment and Collection of Taxes Law: an annual PAYE return filed after November 30, 2026 carries EUR 150 for an individual, EUR 500 for a legal person whose turnover or assets exceed EUR 1 million, and EUR 250 for any other legal person. These amounts may appear modest in isolation, but they accumulate if a series of returns is late, and they create a compliance record that can complicate future dealings with the Tax Administration.

A more significant risk is the best-of-judgment assessment. If a return is not filed at all, the Tax Administration has the power to raise an estimated assessment based on its own view of the taxpayer's likely liability. That figure may be considerably higher than the correctly calculated liability, and it can be more difficult to challenge than a return filed on time. Filing on time — even if the final tax position is uncertain or the supporting figures need refinement — is almost always preferable to missing the deadline entirely. A return can be amended if the numbers need correction; an outstanding filing date cannot be undone.

  • Late filing attracts a fixed penalty per return, separate from any interest on unpaid tax.
  • Late payment of tax due attracts interest at the statutory rate from the payment due date.
  • A best-of-judgment assessment may be raised if no return is filed — this figure is often harder to dispute.
  • File on time even if the final figures are not yet certain — amendments can be made, missed deadlines cannot.
  • Multiple late returns create a compliance record that the Tax Administration may take into account in future reviews.

Individual income tax return dates

Personal filing calendar

July 16, 2026

The annual personal income tax return for salaried employees is filed on Form TD1. Self-employed individuals and those with business income may use a different form depending on their legal structure and whether they are required to prepare audited accounts.

The Tax Department's individual-return page states that tax year 2025 returns are due by October 31, 2026 and are submitted through TAXISnet. The same official page says returns from tax year 2026 onward are submitted exclusively through Tax For All. October 31, 2026 is a Saturday; file in the preceding week rather than relying on weekend system availability. The employer's annual PAYE return, now due November 30, is a separate obligation.

Individuals within filing scope should plan to submit before the deadline, allowing time for TAXISnet access, employer-certificate mismatches or adviser queries.

  • Form TD1 is the standard personal income tax return for salaried employees.
  • Self-employed individuals may use a different form depending on business structure.
  • Track October 31, 2026 as the official 2025 personal return deadline through TAXISnet.
  • The employer's annual PAYE return for 2025 (now November 30, 2026) is separate from the individual's personal return deadline.
  • Allow time before the deadline for system issues or Tax Administration queries.

Corporate provisional tax: two installments

Corporate cash tax planning

July 16, 2026

Companies that expect to be profitable in 2026 are required to pay provisional tax in two equal installments: the first by July 31, 2026 and the second by December 31, 2026. The provisional tax estimate is self-assessed — the company determines its own estimate of 2026 taxable profits and calculates the corresponding tax at 15%. There is no automatic adjustment by the Tax Administration at this stage; the final reconciliation happens when the annual return is filed.

Under-estimating provisional tax carries a specific consequence: if the provisional taxable income estimate is below 75% of the final taxable income, additional tax of 10% applies to the difference between final tax and provisional tax paid or payable, subject to the applicable rules. This is in addition to any interest on the unpaid balance. Finance teams should build a formal review of the provisional tax estimate into their Q2 close process, before the July 31 deadline, to confirm whether the estimate remains reasonable given actual year-to-date performance. A second review before December 31 allows the estimate to be adjusted upward if the year is tracking ahead of the original forecast.

  • First provisional tax installment: due July 31, 2026.
  • Second provisional tax installment: due December 31, 2026.
  • Estimate is self-assessed based on expected 2026 taxable profits at 15%.
  • Under-estimating by more than 25% of the final liability attracts a 10% surcharge on the shortfall.
  • Review the estimate at Q2 close (before July 31) and again in Q4 (before December 31).

Social insurance and GESY contribution deadlines

Payroll contribution calendar

July 16, 2026

Social insurance contributions and GESY (General Health System) contributions are deducted from employee salaries each month and must be remitted to the Social Insurance Services by the employer alongside the employer's own contribution share. These are monthly obligations tied to each payroll run, not annual ones. The employer withholds the employee share from gross salary, adds the employer's own contribution, and remits the combined total. Late payment of social insurance contributions attracts penalties and interest, which can accumulate quickly across a workforce if payroll processes are not tightly managed.

GESY contributions apply at separate rates for employees and employers and are remitted through the same process as social insurance. For salaried employees, the deductions are made monthly and there is no option to defer to a year-end settlement as with income tax. Finance teams should ensure that the social insurance and GESY payment cycle is built into monthly cash flow forecasts and not treated as an incidental item. Late payments in this area are harder to reverse and the penalties are applied per period, not as a one-off charge.

  • Social insurance and GESY contributions are due monthly, not annually.
  • Employer withholds employee contributions from salary and adds its own share before remitting the total.
  • Late payment attracts penalties and interest, applied per missed period.
  • GESY contribution rates for employees and employers are set separately — confirm current rates each year.
  • Include social insurance and GESY outflows in monthly cash flow forecasts as fixed obligations.

Calendar checklist

  • Move the 2025 annual PAYE return to November 30 in your calendar.
  • Schedule the November 10 VAT and November 16 VIES submissions.
  • Confirm beneficial owners with the Registrar by December 31.
  • Assign filing owners for each deadline.
  • Create earlier cut-off dates for support collection.
  • Review VAT notices separately from payroll notices.
